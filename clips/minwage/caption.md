**Title:** What if the federal minimum wage matched inflation every year? 💵

**Caption:**
In 1968 the federal minimum wage was $1.60 an hour, its highest buying power ever. If it had simply kept pace with prices since then, it would be $14.81 today. Instead it's $7.25, and it hasn't been raised since July 24, 2009. 😳

That gap adds up. A full-time worker (2,080 hours) makes $15,080 a year at $7.25, which is below the 2025 poverty guideline for one person ($15,650). At $14.81 that's about $30,800, enough to clear the guideline for a family of three ($26,650).

The numbers:
• Wage history: federal minimum wage effective dates from the U.S. Department of Labor
• Inflation: annual-average CPI-U from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (via FRED)
• "Indexed" wage = $1.60 × CPI(year) ÷ CPI(1968). 2025 average CPI 321.9 vs 34.8 in 1968 is about 9.26×, so $14.81. This is my arithmetic, and it starts from 1968, the year the real value peaked. Starting from the 1938 original ($0.25) the same math gives about $5.71, which is below today's $7.25
• $7.25 in 2025 buys what about $0.78 bought in 1968, about 49% of the 1968 buying power
• $7.25 × 2,080 hours = $15,080. $14.81 × 2,080 = about $30,800. The poverty guidelines (HHS, 48 states and D.C.) are shown for comparison, not as a budget
• "About $154,000": the yearly gap between the indexed and actual wage, times 2,080 hours, added up for 2010 to 2025 in each year's dollars (my arithmetic)
• 30 states and D.C. set a higher minimum than the federal rate; there are no automatic federal increases
• Economists disagree about the effects. CBO's 2021 estimate for raising the federal minimum to $15 by 2025: about 1.4 million fewer jobs and about 900,000 fewer people in poverty

Should the minimum wage track inflation? 👇
