# Sources and assumptions: "What if a Category 5 hurricane hit Miami?"

| Claim in the video | Basis |
|---|---|
| Category 5 = 157 mph or more sustained | Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale (NHC). Category thresholds shown on the HUD: Cat 1 74, Cat 2 96, Cat 3 111, Cat 4 130, Cat 5 157 mph |
| Andrew hit Miami-Dade in 1992 at about 165 mph | NOAA Hurricane Research Division reanalysis, which reclassified Andrew as a Category 5. Other sources cite lower landfall winds (e.g. NPS: 141 mph sustained just before landfall), so "about 165 mph" is the reanalysis peak estimate |
| Storm surge 16.9 ft | Andrew's measured peak in Biscayne Bay (NOAA / hurricanescience.org). The video's surge peaks at about 17 ft |
| Streets about 6 ft above sea level | Many Miami neighborhoods sit around 6 ft (some sources give a higher citywide average); the water depth on screen is surge minus 6 ft, about 11 ft |
| Wind pressure about 65 lb per sq ft | **My estimate:** dynamic pressure q = ½ρv² with ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, v = 160 mph = 71.5 m/s, so q ≈ 3,130 Pa ≈ 65 lb/ft². Real loads depend on gusts, shape and shielding |
| Storm surge 49% of direct U.S. hurricane deaths, 1963 to 2012 | NHC (more recent decades show a lower surge share, mostly because of evacuations and more freshwater-flood deaths) |
| The eye is calm, then the back wall hits from the opposite direction | Standard hurricane structure: wind direction reverses after the eye passes. Eye width is roughly 12 to 30 miles. The duration of calm is not stated in the video |

Illustrative (not data): the compressed time (hours shown as seconds), the exact building and street layout, the window failure and the timing of every event. The scene is labeled "Time compressed · illustrative".
